GroceryPulse — Finding the Clean Control

A from-scratch SEC-filings pipeline across 10 public grocery and retail companies, built to test whether four real strategic bets — automated fulfillment, retail media, store-format changes — actually moved the financial metrics they were supposed to move. The hard part was never running the regression. It was proving the "control" company hadn't quietly made the same bet.

Situation

Grocery companies constantly announce strategic pivots — automated fulfillment, retail media networks, smaller store formats. Each one comes with a story about why it will show up in the numbers eventually. Whether it actually did is a question competitive intelligence teams ask constantly and rarely answer rigorously.

Complication

Naively comparing a company's financials before and after its own announcement conflates the strategy's effect with everything else moving through the sector at the same time — inflation, fuel costs, a pandemic. A valid comparison needs a control group of peers who didn't make the same bet. That's harder than it sounds: in a market this concentrated, competitors are often quietly making similar moves in the same window, which would silently contaminate the exact comparison meant to isolate the effect.

Question

Using only free SEC filings data, could a properly designed difference-in-differences analysis actually isolate whether four real grocery-sector strategic bets moved the metrics they were supposed to move — without quietly fooling itself with a contaminated control group?

Answer

Built a full pipeline from SEC's raw XBRL data (10 companies, hand-verified financial-statement tag mapping, an automated reconciliation check that catches bad derivations before they reach a chart), then ran five separate event studies — not one pooled regression, deliberately, since pooling different companies' different strategic bets into a single staggered-timing model is a known way to bias the result:

  • Every control group was confound-checked against live SEC full-text search, not assumed clean. Testing Kroger's automated-fulfillment bet meant excluding Walmart from the control group — Walmart was running its own competing automation investment in the same window. Testing Albertsons' retail-media launch meant excluding both Kroger and Walmart, since both already had a mature retail-media business years before Albertsons' launch — a confound a less careful design would have missed entirely.
  • The headline finding required no regression at all. Kroger's own most recent 10-K states, in a dedicated footnote, that its automated fulfillment network with Ocado "did not meet operational or financial expectations" — a $2.5 billion impairment charge, including roughly $350 million paid to Ocado just to exit the relationship on closed facilities. The event-study analysis around it is honestly inconclusive on its own (the pre-trend isn't clean, most likely from anticipation effects around the original 2018 announcement) — reported as a real limitation, not smoothed into a false clean story.
  • A follow-up pass through the cash flow statement sharpened that finding. In the same fiscal year, Kroger still paid out $3.58 billion in dividends and buybacks — nearly matching its own $3.86 billion CapEx budget. Its "reinvestment ratio" (the share of CapEx plus shareholder returns that actually went to CapEx) was 51.8%, among the lowest of the 10 companies studied. Albertsons shows a related pattern from a different angle: the most leveraged balance sheet in the group (93.1% liabilities-to-assets) while still running its own $1.8 billion capital-return program. Neither fact proves the capital allocation caused or should have offset the strategic outcome — but both are real, disclosed, and go directly to whether spending matched stated priorities.
A placebo test validated the method before trusting it on the real questions. Splitting the same 10 companies on an economically meaningless axis (alphabetical ticker order) and running the identical event-study code against COVID-19 correctly found nothing — no spurious pattern, no false "effect." Only after the machinery proved it wouldn't manufacture a finding out of noise was it trusted on the four real strategic bets. Two real data bugs surfaced and were fixed the same way along the way: an SEC financial-statement tag assumed universal across all 10 companies wasn't (one company used a different one entirely), and SEC's own period-labeling field turned out to silently misattribute years of financial history unless cross-checked against each filing's actual reporting dates.
FEED 02 — THE ANALYSIS THAT AGREED WITH THE COMPANY'S OWN DISCLOSURE

Kroger vs. a control group with no competing automation bet, centered on the 2021 go-live of its first Ocado-powered fulfillment centers. The pre-period isn't flat — a real limitation, reported as one — and the impairment year's confidence interval is too wide to call on its own. The regression's honest uncertainty and the company's own $2.5B disclosure are two separate facts pointing the same direction, not one inflating the other.

FEED 03 — PROVING THE METHOD BEFORE TRUSTING IT

Same event-study code, same 10 companies, split alphabetically by ticker instead of by any real strategic decision. No pattern — which is the correct result. A method that finds "effects" in a meaningless split would find them everywhere.

FEED 04 — TEN COMPANIES, SIDE BY SIDE

A follow-up pass through the cash flow and balance sheet statements — not just the income statement — for a genuinely per-company view. Two figures are flagged directly on the cards: reinvestment ratio (the share of CapEx + dividends + buybacks that actually went to CapEx, not back to shareholders) and liabilities/assets (balance-sheet leverage, broader than long-term debt alone). Kroger and Albertsons stand out on both, in the same direction.
