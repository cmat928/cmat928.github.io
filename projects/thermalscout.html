Remote Sensing Applied Research Data Fusion

ThermalScout — The Signal That Wasn't

A self-built research pipeline fusing NASA satellite data, a USGS gauge, drifting ocean floats, and species-occurrence records — free tools only, no trial accounts — built to ask what a genuinely merged dataset reveals about a warming lake and the ocean beside it. The two most promising-looking signals in it both turned out to be something other than what they first appeared.

Situation

A tournament-fishing platform side project sparked an interest in NASA's free satellite data — but that platform is a commercial, freshwater-only product, not a sandbox for open-ended exploration. ThermalScout became that sandbox instead: a standalone project to see what a genuinely fused dataset — satellite, in-situ gauge, drifting ocean float, and biodiversity records, spanning both a lake and the ocean beside it — actually reveals, kept deliberately separate from any commercial codebase.

Complication

Every source in a stack like this comes with a hypothesis attached that sounds right until it's actually checked. Standard practice says filtering satellite readings by their own quality flags should make them trustworthy. Ordinary intuition says a biodiversity database's occurrence counts should track real environmental change. Both are the kind of assumption that quietly becomes "settled" the moment you stop at the first result that looks like it confirms them.

Question

What actually survives when each hypothesis is checked against a second, independent source — not computed once and reported, but pressure-tested the way a real finding has to be?

Answer

Two research threads, each pushed past the first plausible-looking result:

  • Satellite temperature, checked against a gauge: MODIS land-surface temperature for Lake Champlain, bit-decoded against its own QC band and validated against a USGS gauge reading. QC filtering caught the single catastrophic day outright — zero valid pixels, correctly refusing a confident wrong answer — but made 5 of the other 9 days worse, not better. On the two days still off by 3°C+ after filtering, every surviving pixel was already flagged at the satellite algorithm's own best-confidence tier, ruling out "filter harder" and pointing to a real physical effect: satellite skin temperature genuinely diverging from gauge bulk temperature, not a fixable data defect.
  • Species records, checked against ocean conditions: Atlantic cod occurrence records jumped from 0 to 32 to 185 between 2024 and 2025, lining up suspiciously well with measured temperature and chlorophyll shifts — until breaking the records down by how each was collected showed almost the entire spike was MATERIAL_SAMPLE data, the signature of one research survey publishing to the database, not a change in the ocean. American lobster's records, by contrast, were 100% direct observation across all six years and showed no such artifact.
Neither hypothesis held up — and that was the actual finding. A pipeline built to confirm the hopeful story would have reported "QC filtering fixed it" and "cod are tracking warming water," then stopped. Checking each claim against an independent source instead turned two expected one-line results into two real, defensible findings about exactly where — and why — each data source can and can't be trusted. Before treating either as final, both were also checked against real peer-reviewed literature spanning ocean remote sensing, marine carbon cycling, and microbial ecology — not to replicate any of it, but to see whether the same shape of caution shows up independently elsewhere. It did, twice.
FEED 02 — WHEN A SPIKE IN THE DATA ISN'T A SPIKE IN REALITY

Same database, same region, same six years — one species' record is a reporting artifact, the other's isn't. Raw occurrence counts are dominated by survey effort until you check, a known problem in biodiversity data that this project's own numbers reproduced concretely rather than just cited.

FEED 03 — WHERE THE PIPELINE FINDS REAL SIGNAL

Not every source needed a caveat. Three independent drifting floats — free, no account, oceanography's own gold standard — show a clean, physically sensible seasonal warming signature with no correction required.
