Why the Naive Comparison Lied

A real 1970s job-training experiment, re-analyzed to show how a plausible-looking comparison group can flip the sign of a program's effect entirely — and exactly how far diff-in-differences can, and can't, fix it.

Naive Estimate
-$8,498
True Effect (Experiment)
+$1,794
Diff-in-Differences
+$3,621

Situation

The National Supported Work (NSW) Demonstration was a real randomized job-training program for structurally disadvantaged workers — long-term unemployed, ex-offenders, welfare recipients. Because assignment to the program was random, comparing treated vs. control earnings within the experiment itself gives a valid causal estimate: participants earned $1,794 more in 1978 than they otherwise would have.

Complication

Most real analyses don't get a randomized control group. The standard workaround is comparing against "similar-looking" people from public survey data instead. Substituting the Current Population Survey (CPS) as a stand-in control for this exact program doesn't just add noise — it flips the sign of the result entirely: the naive comparison says the program decreased earnings by $8,498.

A covariate check shows exactly why. These weren't two similar groups with different treatment status — they were two different populations:

84% Black vs. 7% · 17% married vs. 71% · 77% without a high school degree vs. 30% · 8 years younger on average. Every one of these predicts earnings on its own, with nothing to do with job training.

Question

If a real control group isn't available, can differencing out each group's own pre-treatment earnings trend — diff-in-differences — recover something closer to the true effect than a raw level comparison?

Answer

Partially. Comparing the change in earnings from 1975 to 1978, rather than the 1978 level alone, produces a $3,621 estimate — 4x closer to the true $1,794 effect than the naive comparison, and the correct sign.

But it still overstates the effect by roughly 2x, and the honest finding is why: the pattern is consistent with Ashenfelter's dip — people tend to enroll in job training right after an atypical earnings low, which partially reverts on its own regardless of the program. That inflates the treated group's "before-to-after" change independent of any real effect. The right conclusion isn't "diff-in-differences worked" — it's a specific, defensible account of how far it closes the gap and why it doesn't close all of it.

Bars show each estimation method's result; the dashed line marks the true experimental effect both are trying to recover.
